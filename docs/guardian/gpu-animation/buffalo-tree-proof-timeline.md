# Buffalo camera: tree preparation and fade

The close buffalo camera exposes an avoidable delay after a discontinuous QA camera move in the native Wild Engine island mangroves. The readiness value alone is not proof that a 3D tree is actually drawn. This audit separates the transition controller's verified target from its current blend value, and records both every frame. The frustum-sphere selection is conservative; it does not establish pixel visibility or measure occlusion.

On the GTX 1660 using headless Edge, all 31 selected trees within 90 horizontal units already had a target of 1 in the first captured frame, including a tree 25.16 units away. In the one-second configuration, all 31 were still blending at frame 25 and reached 1 at frame 30. In a separate 0.1-second diagnostic run, all 31 reached 1 at frame 3. The first frame was already partially advanced by earlier setup renders. The test uses a fixed visual delta of 1/30 s after the camera teleport; frame counts are not a wall-clock latency measurement. Reports are `buffalo-tree-proof-timeline/report.json` and `buffalo-tree-proof-timeline-0.1/report.json`.

Three possible approaches remain to compare: prepare and advance the incoming camera's coverage before revealing it; shorten or eliminate the fade only for genuinely close trees after GPU readiness; or retain the current gradual transition and improve camera anticipation. The first can remove visible replacement during a planned transition, but cannot by itself solve arbitrary camera jumps. The second can produce an obvious simultaneous pop and a GPU spike. The third still exposes impostors when preparation or anticipation loses the race. None is accepted for production by these observations alone.

The 0.1-second run shows a shorter exposure, not equivalence: it has not established that the replacement is visually unobjectionable in motion or across biomes and camera paths. Capture sequences, frame pacing and GPU timers under normal gameplay, camera transitions and slow resource preparation before changing the default. The reported render submission durations include this diagnostic setup and are not representative of game-wide CPU or GPU performance.

## Prepared handoff experiment

The loading cinematic already advances verified targets before the world is revealed; the buffalo harness instead teleports its camera without such a handoff. A separate QA experiment advances only targets already covered by the transition controller's GPU-readiness proof. In this camera, all 31 selected trees report full readiness on the first capture and remain so through frame 120. The test then performs a full `World.render(0, 0)` to update packing and draw the prepared state. See `buffalo-tree-prepared-snap-full-render/report.json` and `frame-0.png`. This is still a visible-camera diagnostic with an initial draw before the captured draw, not proof that a hidden handoff occurred or that arbitrary camera movements are solved.

A rejected intermediate experiment advanced the transition, called only `farVegetation.update(0)`, then drew the world directly. Its first image, `buffalo-tree-prepared-snap/frame-0.png`, has missing 3D trunks despite reporting readiness 1; the next ordinary frame restores them. The full render's first image shows the trunks. This demonstrates that the readiness flag is not sufficient to prove draw packing is current. Do not copy the rejected direct-draw sequence into production. Neither experiment measures an uninstrumented frame or GPU cost; slow GPU preparation, actual loading transitions, movement and other biomes remain unverified.

## Actual loading probe

`tools/trees/audit_foreground_tree_live_reveal.mjs` now accepts `QA_SPATIAL_FAR=1` to explicitly activate the spatial far layer in the real application and rejects a run without nearby observed trees after handoff. Without this opt-in, the application currently disables this layer when a spatial session is present unless the `spatialFar=1` URL parameter is supplied; an empty adapter list cannot validate its handoff. The audit also waits for the menu iframe to navigate before dispatching the start event.

On seed 712 in native island mangroves, with the opt-in enabled and the existing loading cinematic unchanged, the first post-handoff observation found 12 of 12 sampled trees within 80 horizontal units at readiness 1; subsequent observations in this run likewise found no sampled unready trees. The image `buffalo-tree-real-loading-spatial-far.png` was captured approximately 1.8 seconds after handoff. The detailed report is `../../lighting/tree-near-retexture/performance/mangrove-foreground-live-reveal-actual-spatial-far-probe.json`. Observations occur at most every 250 ms, use conservative physical/frustum checks and are not per-pixel proof. They cannot exclude a missing or impostor-only frame between samples, and this single startup and camera do not validate gameplay camera jumps or other biomes. Recorded frame intervals include loading, diagnostics and audio, and are not an uncontaminated A/B performance comparison.

With `QA_PER_FRAME_TREES=1`, a separate instrumented run sampled every world update in the first second after handoff and retained four framebuffer images at approximately 0, 0.5, 1 and 1.5 seconds. It recorded 28 updates from 13 to 971 ms; only six contained nearby trees because the camera was initially looking at the sky, and none of their selected trees reported readiness below 1. The images at 1046 and 1518 ms show the forest as the camera lowers (`buffalo-tree-real-reveal-1s.png` and `buffalo-tree-real-reveal-1.5s.png`). The full report is `../../lighting/tree-near-retexture/performance/mangrove-foreground-live-reveal-actual-spatial-far-reveal-sequence.json`. These are four images, not an uninterrupted pixel comparison; per-frame state still does not prove the corresponding geometry was drawn. Readback, checks and sampling perturb timings, so this run cannot measure performance.

## Moving camera baseline

The actual application was run with `QA_SPATIAL_FAR=1 QA_CAMERA_PAUSE=1 QA_CAMERA_PENDING=1 QA_CAMERA_SMOOTH=1 RUN_SUFFIX=camera-pending-smooth-spatial-far`: the simulation was paused and the camera moved for four seconds toward a tree whose initial readiness was zero. The camera moved approximately 196 horizontal units, a deliberately fast stress case, not a measured typical player path. In samples taken approximately every 250 ms, a sample at 1382 ms contained two transitioning trees within 80 units (nearest 76.4, blend 0.67); at 1633 ms it contained ten (nearest 65.0, blend 0.93). Their transition targets were 1 and the prepared standby representation was available, although their native batch was not yet marked prepared. Later samples included a few additional transitioning trees, and the chosen target was ready when first observed within the 80-unit sample radius. The detailed `cameraPause` trace is in `../../lighting/tree-near-retexture/performance/mangrove-foreground-live-reveal-camera-pending-smooth-spatial-far.json`.

This demonstrates that a smooth camera move can encounter partial billboard coverage after the 3D standby is available. It does not prove those trees were unobstructed on screen, determine the exact first or last visible frame, or demonstrate a GPU cost or improvement. The report is instrumented and includes startup, so its overall frame percentiles must not be used as an A/B benchmark. Compare visual continuity and complete CPU/GPU/frame costs of anticipation, a shorter near-only fade, and the unchanged path before accepting a production change; preserve the fallback when no prepared geometry exists.

An independent exploratory run of the same scripted path used `QA_TREE_FADE_SECONDS=0.1`, which intercepts the transition module only in the test browser and leaves GPU-ready requirements intact. Both runs selected `spatial:0:-1:21` and yielded 29 camera samples. The one-second baseline had four samples with partially transitioned trees (15 tree observations, nearest 65 units); the 0.1-second run had none. See `../../lighting/tree-near-retexture/performance/mangrove-foreground-live-reveal-camera-pending-smooth-spatial-far-fade01.json`. The samples are roughly 250 ms apart, longer than the experimental fade itself: missing a transition is expected, not evidence that it became imperceptible. Different runs also have different preparation timing. This is not a controlled visual or performance A/B, and a global 0.1-second fade is not accepted for production.

The comparison was repeated with `QA_CAMERA_EVERY_FRAME=1`, retaining a compact per-update trace in `camera-fade-per-frame-comparison.json`. Both runs again chose the same tree and observed approximately eight seconds of movement and settling. The one-second run had 199 samples, 20 with a partially transitioned tree within 80 units (89 tree observations, nearest 64.3); every partial observation had target 1 and an available standby. The 0.1-second run had 193 samples and no partial observations within that radius. These are separate, instrumented runs, with the same scripted target but not identical preparation timing or exact sample positions. Filtering is conservative and the short fade could occur outside the radius; this is neither proof of continuous pixel visibility nor evidence that a faster swap avoids popping. Per-update diagnostics affect pacing and no GPU cost is measured. Keep the original production setting until visual motion and performance are independently compared.

## Exploratory moving-camera GPU envelope

`QA_CAMERA_GPU=1` disables the per-tree diagnostic while the scripted paused-camera path runs and wraps the normal `World.render` calls in `LoadingGpuQa` elapsed queries. The first four seconds of two independent sessions on the GTX 1660 yielded 98 GPU samples for the one-second fade and 97 for the 0.1-second fade. GPU p50/p95/p99 were 11.91/24.71/30.73 ms and 12.16/25.32/28.32 ms respectively; CPU render-call p95 was 33.08 and 37.38 ms, and intervals between render calls had p95 62.22 and 68.98 ms. Neither run reported disjoint queries; each closed with one pending query. Both selected the same target, but elapsed-time camera movement, resource preparation and sample positions are not exactly matched. The raw retained envelopes and CPU samples are in `camera-fade-gpu-probe.json`.

Two subsequent independent sessions repeated the short and baseline variants, giving the order baseline/short/short/baseline. All four selected the same target, with 95–98 completed GPU samples during the first four seconds and no disjoint or discarded queries; each ended with one pending query. The second short run had GPU p50/p95/p99 12.93/24.12/31.29 ms, CPU p95 35.78 ms and render-call interval p95 63.54 ms. The second baseline had GPU 14.80/26.77/30.21 ms, CPU p95 33.76 ms and interval p95 66.32 ms. The retained `camera-fade-gpu-probe.json` includes all four sample sets. The results overlap and do not establish a reproducible performance improvement from shortening the fade.

This is one ABBA sequence of fresh sessions, not a statistically sufficient campaign or a perfectly matched camera trajectory. Query overhead affects CPU and intervals, and the query covers synchronous rendering, excluding asynchronous preparation and uploads outside the call. It does not establish visual continuity or integrated-GPU behavior. More balanced repetitions, whole-frame measurements and a visual motion check remain required; no production fade setting changed.

## Frame intervals without movement GPU queries

An independent ABBA sequence used `QA_CAMERA_CLEAN=1` to suppress tree-diagnostic scans during the camera movement, without a movement GPU timer or video capture. The application RAF and wall-clock intervals were recorded for the four-second move; the test still has ordinary frame recording, face/audio QA and an instrumented depth-capture hook, so it is not a fully uninstrumented run. All sessions selected `spatial:0:-1:21`, paused simulation and recorded 96–99 frames. Wall interval p95/p99/max in milliseconds were: baseline A1 63.88/79.63/82.13, short B1 65.56/76.75/78.00, short B2 63.66/75.11/78.13, baseline A2 64.98/71.97/75.85. RAF timestamp p95 was about 66.7 ms for every session. Samples and exact camera endpoints are retained in `camera-fade-raf-abba.json`.

These results show no reproducible frame-pacing improvement from the shorter fade in this specific stress path. They do not establish equivalence across hardware or real gameplay, or attribute the remaining long intervals to a particular subsystem. Real-time camera movement and preparation timing remain variable; QA hooks and audio can perturb the result. Do not infer GPU time from these intervals or promote the short fade based on this test.

## CPU stage attribution during movement

An exploratory run with `QA_CAMERA_PROFILE=1` wraps each step of `World.renderFrameUpdates` and `drawRenderFrame` while suppressing per-tree diagnostic scans. The four-second movement contained 99 measured updates. Synchronous CPU p50/p95/p99/max in ms were: far vegetation 5.69/10.79/11.73/12.80; draw submission 8.86/15.13/17.12/19.53; LOD and scene synchronization 3.09/5.21/5.65/8.46; asset-group update 1.61/4.74/5.71/7.69; camera and chunk update 0.41/1.33/5.14/10.16. The trace, stage ordering and timings are in `camera-stage-profile.json`.

`tools/trees/analyze_camera_frame_profile.mjs` aligns the same run's wall intervals with fully contained synchronous stage envelopes (`camera-frame-attribution.json`). In its longest wall interval, 78.73 ms, one measured update accounted for 45.48 ms: camera/chunks 10.16, far vegetation 11.73, asset groups 7.69, draw submission 12.50, and the remaining stages about 3.40 ms. The other 33.24 ms are unattributed: scheduling, asynchronous work, other game/QA work, GPU interaction and measurement overhead are possible contributors. The next longest interval was 71.42 ms with 30.39 ms of contained stage envelopes. These figures do not establish causality, and the remainder is not a GPU measurement. The sampling callback and stage hooks perturb the frame and their timings do not account for work crossing an interval boundary.

A separate `QA_CAMERA_TRACE=1 QA_CAMERA_PROFILE=1 QA_CAMERA_CLEAN=1` run captured a Chromium browser trace during the paused camera path. Its longest observed wall interval was 89.26 ms. An overlapping renderer-main-thread task lasted 84.88 ms and contained a 77.29 ms animation-frame event; the traced `frame` function in `src/app/main.js` lasted 39.08 ms within that event. A 2.42 ms minor GC also occurred inside the broader event. These events are nested and overlap: their durations cannot be added. The trace does not identify the remainder of the animation-frame event sufficiently to call it GPU time, scheduling or a particular application operation. The tracing and CPU-stage instrumentation perturb the result, so it cannot be compared as an uninstrumented benchmark. `camera-browser-trace.json.gz`, `camera-browser-trace-input.json` and `camera-browser-trace-summary.json` retain the trace, clock-aligned frame intervals and longest overlapping events; `tools/trees/analyze_camera_browser_trace.mjs` regenerates the summary.

Another run combined `QA_CAMERA_CPU_PROFILE=1` with browser tracing and aligned V8 samples to the four-second camera move. Of 2,919 movement samples, the largest self-sample category was the generic `(program)` entry (796 samples), which has no attributable application function. Named entries included Three.js `updateMatrixWorld` (90 samples), Three.js `getParameters` (79), `updateAssetLods` (89), `setCoverage` (57), and `standbyTreeKey` (49). Statistical samples are not exact operation durations; time deltas and native/driver waits can be attributed to generic entries or callers. The data suggests examining matrix and material setup alongside vegetation, but it does not explain the unassigned portion of the earlier frame or prove GPU causation. `camera-cpu-trace.cpuprofile`, `camera-cpu-trace.json.gz`, `camera-cpu-trace-input.json` and `camera-cpu-profile-summary.json` retain reproducible evidence; `tools/trees/analyze_camera_cpu_profile.mjs` regenerates the summary. Profiling and tracing perturb the run.

Stack reconstruction identifies 73 of the 79 `getParameters` self samples under `renderer.compile` called by `compileGpuPreparation` during `prepareNativeFarGpu` from `attach-native-far-world.js`; three more follow the standby preparation path. Several `updateMatrixWorld` stacks also lead to the upload draw in that preparation. `renderer.compile` can revisit already compiled variants, so these samples prove preparation traversal and parameter work, not that 76 new shaders were compiled. It executes outside the previously timed synchronous `World.render` envelope, even though its synchronous portions still occupy the main thread. The remaining generic samples and frame time remain unattributed.

Before changing preparation, compare at least three architectures: avoid repeating program traversal when the exact variant and renderer/context generation already have a live readiness proof; consolidate simultaneous species preparations sharing the same scene and recipe; or keep persistent prepared renderables/buffers to avoid repeated setup. Any candidate must still upload changed instance data and fence the exact resources before replacing an impostor, and invalidate proofs on material, lighting, output, disposal or context changes. Account for proof maintenance, memory, loading and travel costs, and validate visual equivalence and context-loss handling. No preparation optimization is accepted from sampling alone.

### Existing same-turn preparation coalescing

The repository already contains an opt-in `SharedNativePreparation` experiment: it joins only same-turn species requests, uploads the union of their textures and resolves each subscriber only after the shared fence; each subscriber still checks its own captured packing. It deliberately does not reuse an in-flight or completed fence for later packing. `QA_SHARED_PREPARATION=1 QA_PREP_PROFILE=1` enables the existing path only during the paused-camera trial and counts synchronous `renderer.compile` calls. In an ABBA sequence of independent sessions, all selected the same tree. During the four-second move, unshared A1/A2 made 392/384 calls taking 362.18/348.39 ms in total; shared B1/B2 made 319/329 calls taking 224.24/253.14 ms. The observed net count of renderer programs added across these calls was zero in each run; this does not prove individual programs were never replaced or compiled.

Wall interval p95/p99 in ms were A1 65.45/80.23, B1 55.35/73.30, B2 53.68/57.24, A2 70.88/77.95; each run recorded 96–104 frames. These are encouraging but instrumented and not a production acceptance: compile wrappers, other QA, audio and real-time camera movement influence the samples, preparation requests can vary, and asynchronous fence wait or GPU execution is not captured by compile timings. `camera-shared-preparation-probe.json` retains call and frame records. Unit tests cover shared admission, cancellation and late subscribers; visual equivalence, context loss, all species/biomes, normal gameplay and an uninstrumented repeated whole-frame/GPU comparison remain required before enabling the shared path.

A second ABBA sequence enabled `QA_CAMERA_CLEAN=1` and omitted both the compile wrapper and movement GPU queries. It retained ordinary frame timing, face/audio QA and the depth-capture diagnostic, so it is not fully uninstrumented. The same tree was selected in all four sessions. Wall interval p50/p95/p99/max in ms were: unshared A1 39.26/64.59/78.37/90.45; shared B1 33.36/51.06/60.59/95.89; shared B2 33.47/56.00/61.73/80.00; unshared A2 37.41/69.37/78.45/80.38. There were 97–112 frames per move. RAF timestamp p95 remained around 66.7 ms in all sessions, consistent with the observed quantization. `shared-preparation-raf-abba.json` retains the samples and endpoints. The lower wall p95 repeated in this limited paused-camera path, while maximums overlap. This is not a guarantee for ordinary gameplay or other hardware; visual continuity, cancellation/context restoration, other biomes and GPU costs remain acceptance gates.

An additional ABBA sequence measured elapsed GPU queries around the synchronous world render while moving. GPU p50/p95/p99/max in ms were: unshared A1 11.91/25.37/29.78/30.60; shared B1 13.48/19.61/22.11/23.16; shared B2 12.49/22.43/26.48/26.59; unshared A2 11.94/21.41/24.99/28.83. Each move had 94–109 completed samples; no disjoint, discarded or overflow-skipped queries were reported, and each session closed with one pending query. `shared-preparation-gpu-abba.json` retains the envelopes. The ranges overlap and show no clear regression inside this measured draw envelope. They exclude asynchronous preparation and upload submissions outside `World.render`, so they do not establish the full GPU cost of sharing. Query overhead, varying real-time trajectories and the paused simulation limit conclusions; other hardware and visual correctness remain untested.

Independent visual recordings with the original and shared preparation are retained as `camera-shared-video-a.webm` and `camera-shared-video-b.webm`; eight stills from each at 1.3–2.0 seconds are `camera-shared-contact-a.png` and `camera-shared-contact-b.png`. The same target was selected. No obvious mass disappearance appeared in the inspected stills, but this is not continuous visual equivalence: frames are sparse, camera poses and readiness timing differ, and dense foliage hides some trees. The recorded state samples included 17 partial tree observations in the original session and 24 in the shared session, with roughly 250 ms sampling; those counts cannot establish a causal difference across sessions. `camera-shared-video-metadata.json` retains the observations. Both videos are instrumented, compressed and unsuitable for performance comparison. More exact fixed-pose comparisons, resource invalidation and context restoration are still required.

This identifies far vegetation and draw submission as places to investigate, not independent causes of every long frame. Timers and other QA hooks add overhead; CPU draw submission may include driver synchronization and is not GPU execution time. Async preparation, uploads outside these synchronous stages, browser scheduling and paused simulation work are not attributed here. The next comparison should examine whether far-vegetation work can be eliminated, simplified or reused during camera motion before proposing caching or parallelization, and must include its maintenance costs and visual behavior.

### Vegetation substage probe

Two additional independent runs wrapped vegetation methods using `QA_FAR_PROFILE=1`; the second also wrapped transition state methods. In 100 world updates during the second four-second move, `FarTreeTransitions.setCoverage` consumed 2.43 ms per world update on average across four adapters, whereas `advance` consumed 0.061 ms. The enclosing `NativeFarLayer.update` averaged 2.89 ms and the sum of four adapter updates averaged 6.17 ms. `NativeFarCoverage.update` averaged 0.35 ms across adapters and standby update 0.76 ms. These numbers are inclusive and nested: they must not be added together. The first run, without transition wrappers, measured 2.91 ms for the layer and 6.20 ms for all adapters. Raw calls are in `camera-far-stage-profile.json`.

`setCoverage` currently revisits the bound population whenever the combined coverage revision changes. Before changing it, compare at least three designs: eliminate unnecessary invalidations by narrowing revisions to actual readiness dependencies; process exact added/removed readiness IDs while retaining a full recomputation fallback; or precompute per-region readiness and update only intersecting regions. Each must preserve suppression, resource and context-loss invalidation and the GPU-backed standby proof. Measure complete bookkeeping, memory, target changes, visual correctness and p95/p99 against the current implementation. The instrumented values are one path with a paused simulation and do not show that any design is faster in production.

### Narrow revision prototype

`QA_JOINT_REVISION=1` replaces the combined revision only in the test browser with one based on native coverage, standby and region revisions, chunk revision, GPU context epoch, and standby source geometry/material identities and versions. The original logic remains the production default. In a separate shadow run, `QA_JOINT_SHADOW=1 QA_FAR_PROFILE=1` recomputed the expected target for every bound tree after each `setCoverage` call: 1,102,512 comparisons over the recorded camera trial produced zero target mismatches. This validates only the observed targets during that path, not all readiness, visible pixels or explicit context-loss/resource-change cases.

An exploratory unshadowed profile on the same selected target found 2.52 ms mean per world update in `setCoverage`, versus 2.43 ms in the earlier instrumented baseline; total four-adapter means were 6.21 versus 6.17 ms. The runs are independent and not an ABBA comparison, but show no reason to accept this revision alone. The candidate is discarded for production; changes in actual coverage may still trigger the full scan. `joint-revision-prototype.json` retains the shadow count and timing traces. The QA interception is reversible and remains available for controlled dependency tests; it is not a production optimization.

### Sparse candidate prototype

`QaSparseTreeTransitions` is an isolated prototype that visits IDs in the active standby bank plus previously positive targets, retaining a complete-scan fallback when the coverage source cannot guarantee a candidate superset. It separately tracks off-region states and preserves the existing proof checks for every candidate. Randomized deterministic unit comparisons cover region rebinding, suppression, coverage revisions, readiness loss and recovery. This remains QA-only; changing candidate completeness could silently leave an incorrect target.

The real mangrove camera trial injected the prototype and exposed standby-bank IDs as candidates. A full target recomputation after each coverage update checked 1,102,512 tree decisions without a mismatch in that run. A separate, unshadowed instrumented run measured `setCoverage` at 1.60 ms per world update versus 2.43 ms in the previous baseline; four-adapter means were 5.33 versus 6.17 ms. `sparse-tree-prototype.json` retains the traces. These are independent single sessions, not an ABBA comparison; the shadow verifies targets, not all rendered pixels, and neither run explicitly exercises context loss, material changes, object removal, loading cancellation or all biomes. Repeat balanced measurements including frame p95/p99, validate the candidate-superset contract and visual equivalence, and exercise those invalidation cases before considering integration.

The CPU stage comparison was extended with a second sparse and then a second original session, for an ABBA sequence on the same selected tree. In the four-second moving interval, `setCoverage` mean/p95/p99 in ms were: original A1 2.43/3.81/4.66; sparse B1 1.60/2.60/3.04; sparse B2 1.64/2.71/3.24; original A2 2.63/3.91/5.53. Combined adapter mean/p95/p99 were A1 6.17/11.69/13.42; B1 5.33/10.00/11.62; B2 5.18/9.55/10.05; A2 6.50/11.45/12.05. Each run contains 97–102 updates. The stage is included within the adapter timings and must not be added to them. This repeats the local saving on one instrumented paused-camera path; camera poses and preparation timing are not identical and no whole-frame or GPU improvement follows from it. Per-update results are retained in the `abba` field of `sparse-tree-prototype.json`.

A separate frame-interval ABBA sequence used `QA_CAMERA_CLEAN=1`, with no movement GPU query or per-tree scan. All sessions used the same selected tree and had 97–101 movement frames. Wall interval p50/p95/p99/max in ms were: original A1 36.03/63.33/68.96/73.81; sparse B1 36.03/68.60/83.75/93.63; sparse B2 38.16/62.04/68.14/75.65; original A2 38.05/64.12/84.98/86.47. RAF timestamp p95 was approximately 66.7 ms in all four. Samples and endpoints are in `sparse-tree-raf-abba.json`.

The local CPU saving did not produce a clear frame-pacing improvement in this small, paused-camera trial; B1 had the largest maximum. Other QA/audio and an instrumented depth hook remain active, the move uses real time and preparation varies, so these are not fully uninstrumented or statistically conclusive results. Do not integrate this prototype on the local CPU result alone. Further visual/resource correctness and representative gameplay measurements are still necessary; retain it as an experiment rather than a production optimization.

## Visual recordings of the moving camera

With `QA_CAMERA_VIDEO=1`, the existing paused-camera trial records the world canvas using `captureStream(30)` and `MediaRecorder`. The independent baseline and short-fade sessions both selected `spatial:0:-1:21`; the retained videos are `camera-video-base.webm` and `camera-video-fade01.webm`, with compact trajectory and readiness metadata in `camera-video-metadata.json`. Both decode at 1280 × 720 and extracted two-second frames show the forest. They are not pixel-synchronized: camera movement follows elapsed real time and recording, loading and preparation vary across sessions. Video compression and 30 Hz capture can hide or introduce apparent changes. These files allow motion review, but extracted stills alone do not establish that the short transition is unobjectionable. No frame-rate or GPU conclusion should be drawn from the recording runs.

`tools/trees/camera_fade_video_contact_sheet.mjs` decodes eight frames at 0.1-second intervals from 1.3 to 2.0 seconds into each recording, producing `camera-contact-base.png` and `camera-contact-fade01.png`. Visual inspection of these sheets did not reveal an obvious mass appearance in that interval. This observation is limited to sparse, scaled frames from one recorded path: a 0.1-second swap can fall between samples, dense foliage masks individual changes, and camera poses differ between recordings. The sheets do not establish continuous visual equivalence or justify deploying the shorter fade.
